# Chapter 6 - Watering

## 6.1 Establishment principle

Newly planted trees depend on moisture within and immediately around their original root system. Even drought-tolerant species require regular establishment watering. The STREAM silviculture handbook recommends watering newly planted seedlings, and the nursery report notes that regular watering helps ornamental saplings pass through the critical adaptation period.

## 6.2 Check moisture before watering

Use a hand trowel, soil probe or finger test 10-20 cm below the surface near the outer root ball.

- **Dry and dusty:** water.
- **Slightly moist and cool:** usually wait and recheck.
- **Wet, sticky or foul-smelling:** do not water; investigate drainage.

Surface appearance is unreliable beneath mulch.

## 6.3 Working water-volume ranges

These are starting ranges, not fixed rules.

### Small shrubs and seedlings

- initial watering: about 5-10 L;
- typical establishment irrigation: 5-15 L per event.

### Small trees

Approximate trunk diameter up to 3 cm:

- 15-30 L per event.

### Medium newly planted trees

Approximate trunk diameter 3-6 cm:

- 30-60 L per event.

### Large transplanted trees

- may require 60-100 L or more per event;
- professional calculation and irrigation design are recommended.

Adjust downward for small root balls and slowly drained clay. Adjust upward for coarse soil, wind and large root balls, provided infiltration is adequate.

## 6.4 First-season schedule

### Weeks 1-2

- inspect moisture daily;
- water when the root zone begins to dry;
- in cool weather this may be every 2-4 days;
- in hot, windy weather it may be more frequent.

### Weeks 3-8

- inspect two or three times per week;
- water deeply as required, often once or twice weekly.

### Remainder of first growing season

- inspect weekly;
- water deeply when the root zone is drying;
- continue during dry autumn weather until the soil begins to freeze.

Never follow a calendar blindly. Rain, soil and wind override the schedule.

## 6.5 Second and third years

Widen the wetted area, reduce frequency gradually, maintain deep irrigation during prolonged dry periods, and prioritize late-season hydration for evergreens before freeze-up without saturating poorly drained soil.

## 6.6 Deep watering methods

### Slow hose

Run a low flow at several points around the root zone. Measure output by timing how long it takes to fill a known-volume bucket.

### Drip line

Use emitters distributed around the root ball and expanding root zone. Move or add emitters as the tree grows.

### Perforated bucket or watering bag

Useful for slow delivery, but remove or inspect bags regularly. Do not allow bark to remain continuously wet.

### Basin irrigation

Useful in permeable soil when a temporary berm holds water. Break the basin after establishment if it causes waterlogging.

## 6.7 Irrigation calculation

`Flow rate (L/min) = container volume (L) / filling time (min)`

`Run time (min) = desired water volume (L) / flow rate (L/min)`

Example: if a 10 L bucket fills in 2 minutes, flow is 5 L/min. Delivering 30 L requires 6 minutes.

For drip systems:

`Total emitter flow = number of emitters × emitter rate`

Four emitters at 2 L/hour provide 8 L/hour; delivering 32 L requires approximately 4 hours.

## 6.8 Soil-specific adjustments

### Sandy soil

Use slower application, consider splitting one event into two cycles, maintain a wide mulch ring, and check deeper moisture.

### Clay soil

Apply water slowly, stop before ponding, irrigate less frequently, inspect for prolonged saturation, and keep the root collar above standing water.

## 6.9 Windy sites

Wind increases transpiration and dries soil. Improve shelter, maintain mulch and inspect more often. Do not compensate for all wind stress by continuously saturating roots.

## 6.10 Evergreen winter preparation

Before soil freeze, provide deep watering if autumn has been dry, ensure drainage, maintain mulch, and install wind or sun protection for vulnerable young plants.

## 6.11 Water quality

Test well water if white deposits, poor infiltration or repeated leaf scorch occurs. Potential problems include high salinity, sodium or alkalinity.

## 6.12 Symptoms

### Underwatering

- dry root ball;
- wilting during cool morning hours;
- leaf-edge browning;
- premature leaf fall;
- weak shoot extension;
- dry, brittle fine roots.

### Overwatering

- persistently wet soil;
- yellowing;
- soft or black roots;
- sour odor;
- poor new growth;
- fungal problems;
- leaf drop despite wet soil.

Always inspect soil before changing irrigation.
