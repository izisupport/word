# Chapter 9 - Pests, Diseases and Diagnosis

## 9.1 Diagnose the site before the pathogen

Many apparent diseases are caused or intensified by drought, waterlogging, deep planting, stem injury, compacted soil, salts, winter desiccation, frost, nutrient imbalance and damaged roots. Begin with the root zone and planting history.

## 9.2 A diagnostic sequence

1. Identify the plant correctly.
2. Determine when symptoms began.
3. Ask whether the entire plant or one branch is affected.
4. Inspect soil moisture.
5. Locate the root flare.
6. Check trunk and branch injuries.
7. Examine leaf or needle pattern.
8. Look for insects, webbing, frass, galleries or scale coverings.
9. Review weather, irrigation and chemical use.
10. Photograph symptoms over time.

## 9.3 Damping-off in nursery seedlings

The Mongolia nursery report illustrates damping-off as a nursery problem and recommends weed control, sanitation and timely fungal-disease response.

Prevention:

- clean trays and tools;
- well-drained medium;
- correct sowing density;
- good airflow;
- avoidance of continuous saturation;
- clean water;
- removal of infected material.

Chemical treatment, if required, must be selected by a qualified professional under current Mongolian regulations.

## 9.4 Needle browning

Possible causes include winter desiccation, drought, root damage, planting too deep, salt, needle-cast disease and insect injury. Uniform browning on the exposed side suggests environmental stress; distinct bands, fruiting bodies or annual progression may indicate disease.

## 9.5 Aphids and sap-feeding insects

Signs:

- curled leaves;
- sticky honeydew;
- ants;
- sooty mold;
- visible colonies.

Management:

- maintain plant vigor without excess nitrogen;
- use a strong water spray on robust plants;
- conserve beneficial insects;
- prune only heavily infested small shoots when appropriate;
- use registered products only when thresholds justify treatment.

## 9.6 Borers

Boring insects often attack stressed trees. Signs include holes, sawdust-like frass, bark cracking, branch dieback and galleries beneath bark. Prevention is primarily stress reduction and avoidance of trunk injury. Advanced infestation may require professional diagnosis.

## 9.7 Root and collar rot

Risk factors include deep planting, continuous irrigation, poor drainage, mulch against bark and damaged roots.

Signs:

- poor growth;
- yellowing;
- branch dieback;
- soft dark roots;
- decayed bark at soil line.

Correct drainage and planting depth. Severely affected trees may not recover.

## 9.8 Powdery mildew and leaf spots

Improve spacing, airflow, sanitation and irrigation timing. Remove heavily infected debris where appropriate. Avoid repeatedly wetting foliage late in cool periods.

## 9.9 Rust diseases

Some rusts alternate between different host plants. Accurate identification is essential before removing plants.

## 9.10 Rodents and browsing

Use guards with enough diameter for air movement. Inspect beneath snow. Do not leave guards tight during trunk expansion.

## 9.11 Mechanical damage

Common sources include mower and string trimmer, vehicle impact, tight wire, dog chains, construction, snow shovel and careless staking. Prevention is more effective than wound treatment.

## 9.12 Chemical safety

Do not recommend a pesticide based only on a symptom photograph. Product registration, active ingredient, formulation, target pest, crop, dose, protective equipment and re-entry period all matter.

Follow this hierarchy:

1. correct species and site;
2. sanitation;
3. irrigation correction;
4. physical or biological control;
5. targeted registered chemical control by label.

## 9.13 When to call a professional

- large dead branches;
- trunk cracks;
- rapid crown decline;
- suspected quarantine pest;
- repeated unexplained mortality;
- work near power lines;
- pesticide use beyond household competence;
- valuable mature trees with structural defects.
