# Chapter 3 - Designing an Attractive and Healthy Yard

## 3.1 Design objectives

A good Mongolian home landscape should reduce wind and dust, provide summer shade without blocking useful winter sun, improve privacy, create year-round visual structure, support birds and beneficial insects, keep roots and branches away from infrastructure, and remain maintainable with available water and labor.

Beauty is produced by composition and health, not by planting the maximum number of species.

## 3.2 Use vertical layers

A resilient planting has several layers:

1. **Tall canopy:** larch, pine, elm or another site-appropriate tree.
2. **Small-tree layer:** rowan, hardy fruit tree or large lilac.
3. **Shrub layer:** caragana, sea buckthorn, spiraea, cotoneaster.
4. **Ground layer:** mulch, hardy perennials or a managed low cover.

Layering reduces wind, creates habitat and prevents the yard from appearing empty below the tree crowns.

## 3.3 Windbreak design

A home windbreak should filter wind rather than stop it abruptly. For a larger property, use a windward row of hardy low shrubs, a middle row of dense medium shrubs or small trees, and a leeward row of taller trees. Stagger plants so gaps do not align. Maintain access for irrigation and pruning.

Do not place the tallest row so close to a building that falling branches, roots or snow accumulation create hazards. Exact spacing depends on mature size.

## 3.4 Shade planning

Place deciduous shade trees where their summer canopy protects windows or seating areas while allowing winter light after leaf fall. Avoid planting directly against a wall.

Evergreens are useful for winter structure and wind filtering, but dense evergreen planting on the south side may block winter sun.

## 3.5 Healthy-air strategy

Trees and shrubs can intercept dust and create psychological and thermal benefits, but they do not replace control of smoke or indoor air pollution. Use rough-textured and dense foliage along dusty boundaries, maintain plant health, combine vegetation with permeable fencing, avoid stagnant pockets beside the house, and keep ash storage away from roots.

## 3.6 Four-season composition

Include at least one feature for each season:

- **Spring:** flowering lilac, apple, cherry or early shrubs.
- **Summer:** shade canopy and green mass.
- **Autumn:** larch color, rowan fruit, sea buckthorn fruit or deciduous foliage.
- **Winter:** pine, spruce, juniper, branch form and persistent fruit.

Repeat a small number of plants rather than using one of everything.

## 3.7 Spacing by mature size

Nursery size is not design size. Research the expected mature canopy. Reserve broad rooting and crown zones for large trees, keep medium trees clear of roofs and wires, space shrub hedges according to mature width, and avoid permanent crowding that requires severe pruning.

## 3.8 Irrigation zones

Group plants by water demand:

- **Low-water zone:** caragana, established sea buckthorn, selected pine and juniper.
- **Moderate-water zone:** elm, lilac, spiraea and many hardy shrubs.
- **Higher-establishment-water zone:** larch, spruce, birch, rowan and fruit trees.

Newly planted plants of every group need establishment water.

## 3.9 Snow management

Do not place fragile shrubs where roof snow or shoveled snow will repeatedly crush them. Avoid piling salt-contaminated snow over root zones. Use flexible hardy shrubs in snow-storage edges.

## 3.10 A simple planting palette for a medium yard

- 2 structural trees: one Scots pine and one Siberian larch;
- 1 small ornamental tree: rowan or hardy crabapple;
- 5-7 caragana shrubs along the exposed boundary;
- 3 lilacs near the living area;
- 5 spiraea in a repeated foreground group;
- 2-3 junipers in a dry rock or gravel zone;
- mulched root zones connected into planting beds.

## 3.11 Design errors to avoid

- planting tall trees beneath wires;
- putting willow or poplar next to pipes and foundations;
- placing spruce in reflected heat without irrigation;
- creating tiny isolated mulch circles surrounded by compacted soil;
- planting a dense wall that blocks all air movement;
- selecting species before assessing water availability;
- crowding plants for an instant effect;
- using lawn irrigation as the only source of tree water;
- burying trunks in raised soil beds.

## 3.12 Final design test

A design is ready when you can explain the purpose, mature size, watering method, winter value and pruning need of every plant, and how people, animals and vehicles move around it.
