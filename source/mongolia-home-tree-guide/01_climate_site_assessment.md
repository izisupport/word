# Chapter 1 - Climate and Site Assessment

## 1.1 Why site assessment matters in Mongolia

Tree survival in Mongolia is often determined less by the nominal hardiness of a species than by the exact planting position. A tree beside a south-facing wall experiences a different environment from the same species in an exposed northwest corner. Important stresses include:

- long, cold winters;
- late spring and early autumn frosts;
- dry air and low annual precipitation;
- intense solar radiation;
- strong wind and wind-driven dust;
- frozen soil while evergreen foliage is exposed to sun;
- short establishment periods;
- compacted or disturbed urban soil;
- alkaline soil and irrigation water in some locations;
- livestock, dogs, rodents and mechanical injury.

A successful design therefore begins with a map of the yard, not a plant catalogue.

## 1.2 Make a simple base map

Record:

- property boundaries;
- house, garage, paths, septic structures and utility lines;
- gates and vehicle movement;
- water source;
- existing trees;
- overhead wires;
- underground pipes;
- snow storage areas;
- dominant winter and summer wind directions;
- sunny and shaded areas;
- low points where water accumulates;
- places where roof runoff reaches the ground.

Mark north. Measure distances rather than relying on visual estimates.

## 1.3 Divide the yard into microclimate zones

### Zone A - Exposed windward edge

Typical conditions:

- strong desiccating wind;
- snow redistribution;
- dust;
- rapid drying;
- high risk of stem movement and root instability.

Use tough shelterbelt shrubs and trees. Establish protection in layers rather than as a single rigid wall.

### Zone B - Sunny south or southwest side

Typical conditions:

- early spring warming;
- high evaporation;
- reflected heat from walls;
- risk of bark sunscald and premature bud activity.

Suitable for drought-tolerant deciduous shrubs and fruiting species where irrigation is available. Young trunks may require winter sun protection.

### Zone C - North or northeast side

Typical conditions:

- cooler soil;
- delayed thaw;
- reduced evaporation;
- possible snow persistence;
- limited direct sunlight.

Suitable for shade-tolerant shrubs and, where moisture is adequate, some spruce or fir. Avoid planting moisture-demanding species beneath roof overhangs where little rain reaches the soil.

### Zone D - Irrigated living area

This is the best zone for ornamental specimen trees, seating shade and flowering shrubs. It should be close enough to the water supply for reliable maintenance.

### Zone E - Wet or low area

Use only species tolerant of periodically wet soil. Do not assume that a low point is always moist; it may be wet during snowmelt and extremely dry in summer.

## 1.4 Test drainage

Dig a test hole approximately 40-50 cm deep in the proposed planting area. Observe:

- soil layers;
- construction debris;
- compaction;
- smell associated with waterlogging;
- visible salts;
- roots from existing plants.

Fill the hole with water and allow it to drain. Refill it and observe drainage. Water that remains for a prolonged period indicates restricted drainage. Extremely rapid disappearance in coarse sand indicates low water-holding capacity.

Do not place a tree in a deep “pot” of amended soil surrounded by compacted clay. Water can collect in the planting pit and deprive roots of oxygen.

## 1.5 Examine soil texture

Use a moist handful of soil:

- **Sand:** gritty, cannot form a stable ribbon.
- **Loam:** forms a weak ball and short ribbon.
- **Clay:** sticky, forms a long ribbon and becomes hard when dry.

Management implications:

| Soil | Main risk | Management response |
|---|---|---|
| Sandy | Rapid drying and nutrient loss | Wider mulch ring, slower deep watering, organic matter incorporated across a broad area |
| Loamy | Usually manageable | Preserve structure; avoid overworking when wet |
| Clayey | Poor aeration and slow drainage | Plant slightly high, widen the hole, avoid excessive amendment, prevent compaction |
| Disturbed fill | Variable drainage and low biology | Replace debris pockets, loosen a broad area, use mulch and gradual soil improvement |

## 1.6 Soil reaction and salinity

A laboratory soil test is preferred. At minimum, request:

- pH;
- electrical conductivity or salinity;
- organic matter;
- available nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium;
- texture.

Do not apply lime to already alkaline soil. Do not add fertilizer merely because a new tree has been planted. Correct a demonstrated deficiency.

White crusting, poor infiltration and leaf-edge burn may indicate salts, but similar symptoms can also result from drought or fertilizer injury.

## 1.7 Assess available rooting volume

A tree needs soil volume, not only a planting hole. Avoid large trees where roots will be confined between concrete, foundations and compacted driveways.

Minimum practical design principles:

- keep large trees well away from buildings and overhead wires;
- reserve narrow strips for shrubs or small trees;
- avoid placing water-seeking species next to drainage or sewer infrastructure;
- do not plant directly over underground utilities;
- maintain access for repair work.

## 1.8 Water-resource audit

Before planting, calculate whether establishment watering is realistic.

Record:

- source: mains, well, stored rainwater or delivered water;
- flow rate;
- hose length;
- storage capacity;
- water quality concerns;
- days when no one is available to irrigate;
- winter drain-down requirements.

A small number of well-watered trees is more sustainable than a large planting that cannot be maintained.

## 1.9 Wind and snow assessment

Observe snow patterns after storms. Deep drifts can protect roots but may break weak branches. Areas scoured free of snow are usually highly exposed and dry.

A permeable shelterbelt is generally better than a solid barrier. A dense wall can create turbulence immediately behind it. A layered planting with shrubs at the edge and taller trees behind reduces wind progressively.

## 1.10 Site-assessment checklist

Before selecting species, answer:

- What is the coldest and windiest part of the yard?
- Where does snow accumulate?
- Which areas receive six or more hours of summer sun?
- Where can water be delivered reliably?
- Does the soil drain?
- Is the soil saline or strongly alkaline?
- What mature canopy and root space is available?
- Are animals or vehicles a threat?
- What maintenance can the household realistically provide?

Only after these questions are answered should species selection begin.
